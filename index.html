Race week protocol
Days 7 through to race gun
›
Days 7–4 before race (Mon–Thu)
Normal taper nutrition. Maintain carb intake — do not reduce just because training volume drops. The muscles are still absorbing the peak stimulus from weeks 8–9.
Days 3–1 before race — Carb load
Target 9–10 g carbohydrate per kg bodyweight — calculated from your body weight above.

Practical approach: add 100–150g extra rice or pasta to each of your three main meals. Reduce fat and fibre to ease digestion and GI comfort on race day. Continue normal protein intake.
Do not experiment with new foods during carb load. Stick to foods your gut knows well.
Race morning · 3 hrs before gun
Pre-race breakfast
150–180g oats OR 3 slices white toast with jam + 1 banana + 250ml sports drink. No high-fibre vegetables. No new foods. No excessive fat.
120–150g carb15g prot
Race fuelling strategy
First gel: km 6 (30 min in) — start early to maintain blood glucose; do not wait until you feel you need it.
Frequency: every 25–30 min thereafter.
Target: 60–90 g carbohydrate per hour (use gels you have trained with).
Caffeine gel: consider one at km 30–35 if trained with caffeine — 3–6 mg/kg body weight.
Fluid: alternate water and sports drink at aid stations — do not take both simultaneously.
Sodium: 500–700 mg/hour minimum — critical beyond 2.5 hrs.
You have one marathon in your legs — you know how your body responds at km 30+. Use that experience: if you bonked last time, add 15–20g more carbs per hour from Week 8 onwards. If GI was an issue, switch to chews or a liquid carb source.
Caffeine strategy
Dose: 3–6 mg per kg bodyweight (86 kg → 260–520 mg). Start at the lower end if caffeine-sensitive.
Timing: 45–60 min before the gun — caffeine peaks in plasma at ~60 min.
Source: caffeine tabs or a strong coffee (~80mg/cup). Avoid new sources on race day.
Withdrawal trick: cut caffeine 7–10 days before the race, then reload on race morning — the re-sensitisation can meaningfully amplify the performance benefit.
Only use caffeine if you have practised it in training. GI distress is a real risk on race day if your gut isn't adapted.
Post-race recovery
Within 20 min of finishing: recovery shake or banana + milk. Within 2 hrs: full meal with protein and carbs. Prioritise rehydration with electrolytes — not plain water — for the first 2 hours.

Phase-by-phase emphasis
Nutrition priorities shift as training shifts
›
Phase 1 · Foundation (Weeks 1–3)
You have one marathon completed — your gut microbiome and aerobic base are already adapted to long-duration fuelling. Use this phase to lock in meal timing around sessions and refine your gel tolerance. Hit protein every day. Begin practising gel intake on Saturday runs from Week 1, not Week 4.
Phase 2 · Build (Weeks 4–7)
Fuel the quality sessions. Never run intervals or threshold in a depleted state. Increase carbs on Tue/Thu/Sat. Begin race-day simulation: gels every 40–45 min on long runs, target 60 g/hour. Test electrolyte products.
Phase 3 · Peak (Weeks 8–9)
Protect recovery between sessions. Post-session carb+protein window is non-negotiable. Sleep 8–9 hrs is as important as any meal. Do not cut calories — this is the highest energy demand period of the 12 weeks. Build in-run carbs to 90 g/hour. Protein: consider increasing to 2.2–2.4 g/kg during peak weeks — back-to-back high-intensity sessions create the highest muscle repair demand of the entire program.
Phase 4 · Taper (Weeks 10–12)
Do not reduce carbohydrates proportionally with the training volume cut. The muscles are still absorbing the peak stimulus. Maintain carb intake, slightly reduce total calories by trimming fat. Begin the full 3-day carb load in the final days before the race.
Gut Training Protocol — Progressive Carb Adaptation
A significant proportion of recreational marathon DNFs are GI-related. The gut adapts to high-carb intake just like muscles adapt to training — it requires consistent practice. GI distress at 90 g/hr in Week 1 is normal; GI comfort at 90 g/hr in Week 9 is the goal.
Phase 1 (Weeks 1–3) · 45–60 g carbs/hr
Start with 1 gel every 45 min on long runs. Use the brand you will race with — gut adaptation is brand-specific. Never skip fuelling on runs over 60 min.
Phase 2 (Weeks 4–7) · Build to 75–80 g/hr
Add a second carb source (e.g. dates + gel = mixed glucose and fructose sugars). Mixed-sugar products increase maximum gut absorption. Never skip fuelling on runs over 60 min.
Phase 3 (Weeks 8–9) · Target 90 g/hr
Use 2:1 glucose:fructose ratio products. Two gut transporters absorb glucose and fructose via separate pathways — mixing them unlocks the 90 g/hr ceiling. Train the gut to absorb this consistently through repetition.
Race Day · Execute exactly what you practised
Stick to every product, timing, and volume you trained with. Never try a new product on race day — not a new gel brand, new electrolyte, new volume, or new timing.

Iron & Ferritin — Runner Priority
The most common hidden performance limiter in distance runners
›
Foot-strike haemolysis during running destroys red blood cells with each footfall. Combined with sweat losses, distance runners are at high risk of iron depletion even without obvious symptoms.
Optimal ferritin for performance
>50 µg/L minimum; ideally >80 µg/L. Below 30 µg/L will measurably impair VO2max and running economy.
Target
Symptoms of latent deficiency
Elevated HR at same effort · unexplained performance plateau · persistent fatigue not resolved by rest. These symptoms appear before anaemia — ferritin can be dangerously low while haemoglobin is still normal.
Watch for
Test frequency
Every 8–12 weeks during heavy training — not just annually. Request ferritin specifically; standard iron panels often miss early deficiency.
Supplementation (if confirmed deficient)
Ferrous sulfate 100–200 mg · taken with Vitamin C (100 mg+) to enhance absorption · away from coffee, tea, and calcium by at least 2 hours · take on alternate days — daily dosing reduces absorption via hepcidin upregulation.
Food sources
Lean red meat 3–4×/week · lentils and legumes · dark leafy greens (spinach, kale) · fortified cereals. Haem iron from meat absorbs 3× more efficiently than non-haem iron from plants.
Do not supplement iron without confirmed bloodwork. Excess iron is pro-oxidant and harmful. Always test first.

Non-negotiables
The rules that matter most
›
Always eat post-session
Skipping the post-session carb+protein window is the most common mistake concurrent athletes make. It directly impairs the next session.
Hydrate continuously
Aim for pale yellow urine by mid-morning. Do not rely on thirst — by the time you are thirsty you are already 1–2% dehydrated and performance is declining.
Practise race nutrition in training
Every gel brand, electrolyte tab, and fluid volume must be tested on long training runs. Race day is never an experiment.
Do not undereat during taper
The most common race-week mistake. Training volume drops but the muscles are still loading glycogen from weeks of peak work. Feed them.
Protein every single day
Muscle protein synthesis and connective tissue repair happen primarily during rest, not during training. Protein target is not negotiable — not on rest days, not in taper — calculated from your body weight above.
Sleep is nutrition
GH and testosterone peak during slow-wave sleep. 8–9 hrs in peak weeks (8–9) matters as much as any food strategy. Prioritise sleep over extra training volume every time.
